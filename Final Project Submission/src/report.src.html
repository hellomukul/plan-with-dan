2Motivation: who has this problem?

I'm a first-year in SOM's MBA Class of 2028, and choosing electives here is a planning problem with more moving parts than it looks. Every full-time MBA student solves it before each term, and the rules that decide whether a plan works live in different places:

  • Unit rules. 72 units to graduate (about 30.5 core, at least 41.5 elective). Each of the first three terms needs at least 16 graded units, and a heavy term doesn't make up for a light one. More than 24 units in a term needs approval. (Bulletin, degree requirements)
  • Half-term clashes. Terms are split into session-1, session-2 and full-term courses. Overlapping courses aren't allowed, and the registrar removes one after add/drop. Changes after add/drop use a late form and a $25 fee. (Bulletin, course policies)
  • Two graduation requirements with hidden budget rules. Global Studies (one of six options) is paid for from a 10-day Global Studies Account that is never topped up, and an International Experience needs all 10 days unused. Leadership needs one approved elective. (SOM Global Studies Requirement)
  • An auction with no price signal. Electives are allocated by bidding points. SOM doesn't publish past clearing prices; the 2026–27 auction runs Nov 9 to Jan 4.

How students deal with it today, and what it costs

Today a student stitches together the public SOM course directory, the auction app (NetID login), the Bulletin, the internal site, the academic calendar, Yale Hub for add/drop, and usually a spreadsheet. A student built MySOMClasses because no official planner existed. ChatGPT can talk about goals, but it doesn't have SOM's current catalog, so it can invent courses and times.

  • Time. A first-year planning the rest of the degree has 159 electives open to them across the four quarters left (counted by the app's own retriever for a first-year MBA, Oct 25). Reading those descriptions against a goal, then checking each candidate's session and times against the cohort's core, takes hours. My estimate is 2–3 hours per planning round (method in Section 7). This is an estimate, not a measurement.
  • Money and seats. A missed clash surfaces after add/drop, when fixing it means the $25 late form, and bid points spent on the wrong course are gone.
  • Frustration. A 2018 SOM award winner described the auction as "causing enormous stress, anxiety, and frustration" (Poets&Quants).

Two classmates showed me the problem is real. A first-year MBA in the Silver cohort had planned 32 units in Fall 2027, eight over the 24-unit limit, and asked for a way to move courses around. A second-year MBA told me they had planned to build a similar tool themselves and never found the time.

Why it matters: SOM has roughly 700 full-time MBA students at any time (about 350 per class across two classes; that's my approximation, not an official count). Each one repeats this before every term, every mistake is found late, and every student's goal is different, so copying a friend's schedule doesn't work.

What Plan with Dan does that none of them do. It works from a goal written in the student's own words to a plan they can bid on, and every step is grounded in SOM's real data:

  1. Goal → ranked courses for every quarter left. Elihu, Dan's course-picking owl, ranks real SOM courses for the goal and places each in the earliest quarter where it fits the student's week, through Spring 2028 for first-years. Each pick quotes its own course description as evidence. Courses at other Yale schools appear separately, labelled with their school.
  2. Code, not the model, decides what's true. Clashes (half-terms included), units, the 16–24 unit range, Leadership, Global Studies and the 10-day budget are computed in Python. The model can't invent a course: code drops any pick that isn't on its shortlist.
  3. A chief of staff, not a chatbot. Dan's Desk tells the student their next step and the few dated decisions that matter this week; Ask Dan routes any SOM question to one specialist with tools and shows a receipt.
  4. The auction, honestly. Point splitting, crowdsourced bid outcomes (hidden until five different students report a course), a result form, and help with a body-to-body seat swap.

The closest alternative, and why the difference matters. CourseTable is the best Yale-wide tool and I use it. It answers "when is this course?". Plan with Dan answers "which courses get me to my goal, and does that plan satisfy SOM's rules and auction?" ChatGPT can hold that conversation, but without SOM's data it can't be trusted on a single course number. Here the agents only reason; tools supply every fact.

What I built on top of the class base. The project started from the class's lecture 8 Yale SOM course explorer: a catalog browser with one Q&A agent on gpt-6-astra that sent the whole catalog to the model on every search. I kept the course data idea and the Handsome Dan mascot and rebuilt the rest: accounts and saved plans; a dispatcher with six specialists, Elihu, the Chief of Staff, the Memory Keeper and the transcript reader; 15 tools; quarter-aware clash logic (the base app treated "spring" and "spring-1" as no clash); the safety layer; per-call token accounting; two-year planning; the Desk; calendar sync; requirements; bidding; and 138 automated tests.

4The agents

The app has 11 AI agents in four flows. Each agent is a PydanticAI agent on gpt-6-luna. Its instructions are one shared primer (grounding and safety rules, identical for every agent) followed by its own role section. Plain code does everything that can be computed: retrieval, clash checks, units, requirements, budgets, placement and fact-checking.

Design choices, and why

  • A dispatcher, not a boss. In class we built a manager agent that calls several specialists and reads all their reports. Here a cheap dispatcher (about 2.2k input tokens, $0.00016–0.00018 per turn, measured) sends each turn to one specialist that carries only its own tools. That means fewer tokens per turn and fewer chances for an agent to wander outside its lane.
  • The AI reasons; code computes. The model never does unit math or remembers a time. Fifteen tools return facts from SOM's data and the 2026–27 Bulletin, and the primer forbids stating anything a tool didn't return.
  • Code before and after the model. In Course picks a BM25 retriever cuts 159 eligible courses to 36 before Elihu sees them; afterwards a validator and a placer run in code. Only one step spends tokens.
  • The human stays in charge. Agents can only propose (suggest_add); nothing changes until the student clicks, and every change has Undo.
  • Model: gpt-6-luna. It's the only model allowed on the course key. At $0.10 input / $0.01 cached / $0.50 output per 1M tokens it is 100× cheaper than gpt-6-astra, which the class base app used. Tool calling, structured outputs and image reading (the transcript reader) all worked in testing. The shared primer sits first in every agent's instructions so the provider can serve it from its prompt cache.

One real run, step by step

Test student: a first-year MBA in the Silver cohort with a healthcare-consulting background. Preferences: nothing before 9 a.m., Fridays free. These are real runs on Oct 25, 2026, on a local copy of the app (same code as the live site) with a scratch database.

  1. The student types a goal on Course picks: "Move into healthcare private equity and understand AI strategy." Screen runs the same injection check as chat: passed, $0.
  2. Retriever builds the pool: every course open to an MBA first-year, not core, not taken or planned, in a quarter they can still plan (Fall 2026 to Spring 2028). Result: 159 eligible → 36 shortlisted by BM25 against the goal, so about 47.7k tokens of course text are never sent.
  3. Elihu ranks the shortlist: 7,534 input tokens (1,826 cached), 1,405 output, $0.00129, 11.7 s. It returned 12 courses: MGT 846 Introduction to Private Equity (essential), MGT 847 Leveraged Buyouts, MGT 948 Security Analysis & Valuation, MGT 698 Healthcare Policy, Finance, and Economics, MGT 860 Generative AI for Managers and more, each with a quote such as "a holistic view of the Private Equity business from a practitioner's perspective".
  4. Validator checks every key against the shortlist and every quote against the real description: kept 12, dropped 0. (In production it has dropped invented picks 8 times; see Safety.)
  5. Placer puts each course in the earliest quarter it is offered that fits the week and has room: MGT 846 in Spring-1 2027 (Mon 4:10–7:10), MGT 847 and MGT 948 in Fall 2027 as expected courses, because SOM hasn't published 2027–28 yet. The page says so: "This is based on the current year's course offerings." Separately, code adds four "Beyond SOM" matches from other Yale schools.
  6. The student keeps the courses Elihu ticked and clicks "Add to my plan" (0.5 s). My plan & timetable now shows them in their terms with Undo for each.
  7. A follow-up in Ask Dan ("Find me a spring elective on healthcare strategy that fits my schedule") goes Dispatcher → Course Scout, which called search_catalog three times, course_details and check_fit four times each, then suggest_add. It recommended MGT 878 and caught that MGT 995 now clashes with the MGT 846 the student had just added. Cost $0.0017.

The Desk run. For the same student, the desk scan (code) produced three cards: "Add 3 more units to Spring 2027, due Jan 26" (13 of the 16-unit minimum, all core), "Pick a Global Studies path, due Mar 8" and "Plan a Leadership course". The Chief of Staff wrote the brief from those cards only: 1,939 input tokens (1,545 cached), 252 output, $0.00018. Only one model-written brief is made per student per day, and later loads are free.

Malfunctions I found by testing, and fixed

  • History poisoning. A blocked injection was saved to chat history and replayed into the next prompt, which tripped Azure OpenAI's content filter and broke the following turn. Fix: blocked turns are excluded from history, and provider filter errors are caught, logged and answered politely.
  • Budget overrun inside one call. The first news answer ran 11 searches and cost $0.116, over the $0.05 turn cap, because the cap is checked between calls. Fix: the search cap, a 3-request limit, money reserved for searches up front, and an over_run_budget audit event.
  • Out-of-list recommendation. The Curator returned MGT 674, which wasn't in its shortlist. The Validator dropped it every time.
  • Wrong program's section. An MBA student was given an EMBA-only section. Fix: sections are filtered by program.
  • Overloaded term (from a real user). A first-year had 32 units in Fall 2027. Fix: the unit bars on every term, and Move/Remove with Undo on each course.

Scale, cost and reach

  • Hours. If it saves about 2 hours per planning round, roughly 700 full-time MBAs (approximate) and two rounds a year come to about 2,800 student-hours a year (700 × 2 h × 2). The AI cost is about $96 a month in planning months, at most about $1,150 a year even if every month were a planning month.
  • Quality. Every pick is a real SOM course with a quoted reason; clashes and unit limits are caught before bidding rather than after add/drop; the Global Studies budget rule is checked for every option.
  • Now possible. Planning both years at once (expected 2027–28 courses, clearly labelled as a forecast) and seeing courses from other Yale schools next to SOM's, which no existing tool shows an SOM student.
  • Live usage so far (production database, counts only, Oct 24–25): 7 accounts (4 with yale.edu emails, all 7 onboarded; one is mine), 5 active in the last 7 days, 35 courses planned by 4 students, 15 questions to Dan from 4 students, 4 Course picks packages. This is early and small, and it includes my own testing.

8Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Only two real users, and no timed test. The time savings are an estimate. The first-year's 2027–28 plan also showed that the 2027–28 courses are a forecast from this year's offerings (SOM's feed has no courses yet for Fall 2027 or Spring 2028); the app labels them, but they will change.
  • Time preferences are a soft rule. Course picks prefers sections inside the student's hours, but when a course's only section breaks them it still offers it. A test run caught MGT 635 (8:30 a.m.) placed for a "nothing before 9" student with no warning; the card now carries an amber "Outside your preferences: starts before 9:00 AM" badge, with a test. It still doesn't swap in a different course on its own.
  • No real bid history. SOM doesn't publish clearing prices. The app crowdsources outcomes, hides them until five different students report a course, and never shows demo rows as real data.
  • Rules are transcribed and some flags inferred. Leadership courses come from the MGLD flag in SOM's public feed, and the app tells students to confirm on the internal list. Requirements follow the 2026–27 Bulletin.
  • No course evaluations or workload data (behind Yale login). "How it's graded" is read from descriptions, and 100 of 273 electives say something.
  • Courses at other Yale schools come from the public Bulletin, without meeting times, so they can't be clash-checked until the student types the time in.
  • Operations. Free hosting sleeps after quiet periods; there's no password reset by email (Google and Microsoft sign-in are the fallback); AI news depends on web sources of mixed quality.

With another month

  1. A timed user study with 8–10 students across MBA, MAM, EMBA and international students (the second-year's advice: "ask diverse people"), measuring time to a clash-free plan by hand vs in the app, and how many picks they would actually bid on.
  2. An evaluation set of 50 student goals with hand-labelled good courses, to measure Elihu's ranking quality and catch regressions when prompts change.
  3. An auction "what-if": a pre-auction simulator using crowdsourced bids, and Shortlist compare for courses at other Yale schools (today it compares SOM courses only).
  4. Data partnerships: ask the registrar or student government for anonymised clearing prices and course evaluations, and Yale-wide meeting times for cross-school clash checks.
  5. Yale sign-in (CAS) and a cohort view ("which friends are in this course").
