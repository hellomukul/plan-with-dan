1 · Motivation: who has this problem?

Every SOM student, twice a year. Before each term, roughly 700 full-time MBA students (plus MAM, MMS and EMBA students) build a schedule out of ~100 eligible electives per term. They bid scarce points in SOM's course auction and make sure the plan satisfies degree rules that live in four different places. I am one of these students: planning means juggling the SOM course directory, the auction app, the academic calendar, the Bulletin's degree requirements and usually a spreadsheet.

Your own story (edit before submitting): one or two sentences about a real planning moment, e.g. how long your last bidding round took or a clash you hit.

How it's done today, and what it costs

  • Information is scattered. Course times are in the public directory, the auction in a separate NetID app, requirement lists on the internal site, Global Studies rules on another page, and deadlines on the calendar. A student built MySOMClasses precisely because no official planner existed.
  • Hidden clashes. SOM terms are split into session-1, session-2 and full-term courses. Overlapping courses are not allowed, and the registrar removes one after add/drop. Late changes cost a $25 fee (course policies).
  • Bidding anxiety with no price signal. SOM does not publish past clearing prices. A 2018 SOM student leader said the auction was "causing enormous stress, anxiety, and frustration throughout the student body" (Poets&Quants). The Yale Daily News reported a course with 126 bids for 70 seats.
  • The Global Studies trap. Every student has a 10-day Global Studies Account that is never topped up. An International Experience needs all 10 days unused, and students pay their own airfare (Bulletin). Spending 5 days on a GNW in year 1 silently rules out an IE in year 2.
  • Choosing by popularity instead of goals. With limited time, students copy friends' schedules rather than mapping courses to their career goal.

The cost is mostly time and bad outcomes: hours per term per student, wasted bid points, missed requirements, and courses that don't serve the student's goals. At SOM's scale, even one hour saved per student per term is ~1,400 student-hours a year.

What Plan with Dan does that none of them do: it is a chief of staff for SOM decisions, not another inbox summary. Every morning, plain code scans the student's plan, requirements, deadlines, bids and Global Studies budget and produces at most three decision cards, each with a real due date and a cited source; an agent only words the brief. On top of that it combines (1) an AI recommender that can only recommend real SOM courses and must quote the description to justify each pick; (2) clash checking on real session dates (half-terms included) with same-slot backups; (3) SOM-specific requirement tracking, including the 10-day GSA budget rule that most students don't know about; (4) a bid coach that is honest about missing data and crowdsources real outcomes for next year; and (5) one private calendar feed for Google, Outlook and Apple. It started from the class's lecture 8 course explorer: I kept the course data and the mascot and rewrote the backend and UI (see "What I added", Section 8).

Design choices (and why)

  • Dispatcher, not a boss. In class we built a boss that calls several specialists and reads all their reports. Here a cheap dispatcher (~1.3k tokens, $0.0002) sends each turn to one specialist that carries only its own tools. That is fewer tokens per turn and fewer chances for a specialist to wander outside its lane.
  • The AI decides; code computes. 13 tools are plain Python: clashes use real session dates, units and requirements follow the 2026–27 Bulletin, and the GSA budget rules are encoded. The model reasons about goals and trade-offs; it never does the math or remembers times.
  • Code before and after the LLM in the recommender. A BM25 retriever narrows ~100 eligible courses to 28 before the Curator runs. Afterwards, a validator, a scheduler and backup finding run in code. Only the Curator and Critic spend tokens.
  • Chief of staff, but code decides what matters. The desk scan (clashes, the 16-unit minimum, SOM deadlines, Global Studies, Leadership, bid split, missing times, missing goals) is deterministic and every card cites its rule; the agent can't invent a date. Students approve, snooze (3 days) or dismiss each card, and every plan change is logged with undo.
  • Human in the loop. Agents can only suggest a course (suggest_add); the student clicks "Add".
  • Model: gpt-6-luna through Portkey. It is the only model allowed on the course key, and at $0.10 / $0.50 per 1M tokens it is 100× cheaper than gpt-6-astra. Structured outputs and tool calling worked reliably in all test runs.

One real run, step by step

Run A: chat. The student (first-year, Silver cohort, healthcare-consulting background, goal: healthcare PE internship, then running a healthcare services company) asks "Which Global Studies option fits me best, and does it fit my GSA days?"

  1. Safety screen (code): passes the message; spend guard confirms budget left.
  2. Dispatcher returns {specialist: "planner", task: "…"}.
  3. Planner calls my_profile_and_plan (year 1, 0 GSA days used), requirements_status (13 Spring units, LDR met, GSR not met), global_options (all six options with budget checks and matching Spring courses) and plan_review (no clashes).
  4. Output check (code) verifies every MGT number mentioned exists in the catalog, then the answer and its receipt are saved.

Run C: Dan's Desk. When the same student opens the app, the desk scan finds three decisions (Spring is 3 units short of the 16-unit minimum, Fall session-2 add/drop closes Nov 2, and four bid courses have no point split). The Chief of Staff agent turns them into a brief (1,329 input / 86 output tokens, $0.00018), and the second load that day is served from cache at $0.

Malfunctions I found by testing, and fixed

  1. History poisoning. The blocked injection text was saved to chat history and replayed into the next prompt, which tripped Azure OpenAI's content filter and broke the following turn. Fixed: blocked turns are excluded from history, and provider filter errors are now caught, logged and answered politely.
  2. Budget overrun inside one call. The first news answer ran 11 web searches and cost $0.116, more than twice the $0.05 turn cap, because the cap is checked between calls. Fixed with the search cap and a 3-request limit (now about $0.04), plus an over_run_budget audit event.
  3. Wrong section for the program. The scheduler assigned an EMBA-only section (§E1) to an MBA student. Fixed: section choice is now filtered by the student's program.
  4. Silent truncation. A 20,000-character cap silently cut the Curator's shortlist in the full-catalog experiment. Removed, because the shortlist size is already bounded in code.
  5. Uncited news. Added a strict citation rule plus a code check that warns the student when sources are missing.

Simulated persona testing (not a substitute for the pilot). Six AI tester agents each played a different student (an anxious first-year on a phone, a second-year power user, a budget-minded international student, EMBA/MMS students, a security tester, a career switcher) against the real app for about $0.16 in total. They found 8 serious bugs that every other test had missed, including first-year spring core not being counted toward units, chat proposing another program's section, stale Global Network Week listings and signup timing. All were fixed and covered by 14 new tests (docs/persona_testing.md). A follow-up audit against the 2026–27 Bulletin extended the rules to every SOM program (MBA for Executives, MAM, all five MMS tracks, joint degrees); MAM students, for example, went from seeing 2 courses to 88 (docs/programs_data_audit.md).

If one hour per student per term is saved, SOM's ~700 full-time MBAs save ~1,400 hours a year for about $3,000 a year in AI cost at the assumptions above. The bigger win is quality: plans that actually map to each student's goals and never break the rules.

7 · Limits and next steps

  • No real bid history yet. SOM doesn't publish it. The app crowdsources outcomes; demo rows are labelled SAMPLE. Next: ask SOM's registrar or student government to share anonymised clearing prices.
  • Rules are transcribed. Requirements and dates follow the 2026–27 Bulletin; LDR/GSR flags are inferred from SOM's public feed codes. Next: a monthly refresh script and a "report an error" button.
  • No course evaluations or workload data (behind Yale login). Next: a partnership with CourseTable or SOM.
  • Calendar sync is one-way (subscription feed). Google refreshes slowly (up to ~24 h). Full API sync would need OAuth keys and Google verification.
  • Free hosting sleeps, and its disk resets. Use Neon Postgres via DATABASE_URL for durable accounts.
  • Next month: Yale CAS sign-in; a cohort view ("which friends are in this course"); a pre-auction "what-if" simulator; Spring core cohort mapping; and an evaluation set of 50 student profiles to measure recommendation quality.

8 · What I added to the lecture 8 base

The lecture 8 Yale SOM Course Explorer was a catalog browser with one Q&A agent (gpt-6-astra) that sent the whole catalog to the model on every search. I kept the SOM course data and the Handsome Dan mascot and rewrote the rest:

  • The 8-agent crew (dispatcher pattern) and the 5-stage recommender.
  • Student accounts with saved profiles, plans, Global Studies choices, chats and bids.
  • Deterministic planning tools that fix the base app's half-term clash bug.
  • A calendar feed; the safety layer; per-run token accounting; 58 tests.
  • Seat limits and requirement flags merged from SOM's public feed.
  • Dan's Desk: a chief-of-staff home with a code desk scan, a cached Chief of Staff brief, honest best fits and an undo log.
  • A research-driven redesign ("Quiet Yale"): after the first version felt busy, I studied Linear's calm redesign, NN/g's visual-hierarchy and dashboard research, and Google's People + AI Guidebook on explaining recommendations. Result: one Yale-blue accent, Geist type, no emoji, bars instead of rings, a ⌘K command menu, and fit labels instead of percentages.
  • Privacy controls: export, delete, a privacy page, CSP, generic signup.

How to run: see the README (pip install -r requirements.txt, then uvicorn backend.main:app; the .env is read from the repo folder, then the folder above it). Sources for every SOM rule are in backend/rules.py and docs/research.md. Not an official Yale tool.
